Question 1
Russell defines intelligence as a relationship among three things. Which of the following best captures his definition?
  • AIntelligence is the ability to pass a Turing test
  • BIntelligence is measured by IQ scores across cognitive tasks
  • CAn entity is intelligent to the extent that what it does is likely to achieve what it wants, given what it has perceived
  • DIntelligence is the capacity for abstract reasoning and language
Russell's definition connects perception, action, and objectives: "an entity is intelligent to the extent that what it does is likely to achieve what it wants, given what it has perceived." All other characteristics of intelligence — thinking, learning, inventing — serve this core capacity.
Question 2
How does E. coli demonstrate a primitive form of intelligence?
  • AIt learns from past mistakes and avoids poison
  • BWhen it senses increasing glucose, it swims longer and tumbles less — its behavior varies with what it perceives
  • CIt communicates with other bacteria to find food collectively
  • DIt uses a simple neural network to navigate
E. coli's behavior varies according to its environment — when it senses increasing glucose concentration, it swims longer and tumbles less. This fits Russell's definition: what it does (swim toward glucose) achieves what it wants (more glucose), given what it perceives (increasing concentration). It has no brain — just chemical reactions — but it has a basic form of intelligence. However, it never learns.
Question 3
What is the "Baldwin effect" and why does it matter for intelligence?
  • ALearning changes DNA directly, speeding up genetic evolution
  • BIntelligent organisms tend to develop larger brains over time
  • CEvolution favors organisms that resist environmental change
  • DThe capacity for learning speeds up evolution because the organism fills in details that evolution would otherwise have to specify
The Baldwin effect (proposed in 1896) explains how learning accelerates evolution: if evolution only needs to specify "472***" and the organism learns the last three digits during its lifetime, evolution's job is much easier. Learning doesn't change DNA directly, but it acts as an evolutionary shortcut. Culture accelerates this further.
Question 4
Russell discusses the brain's reward system. What is the evolutionary problem he identifies with reward signals, and what example illustrates it?
  • AThe reward system is too slow, making humans poor at avoiding danger — illustrated by snakebite deaths
  • BThe reward system only works for immediate gratification — illustrated by savings rates
  • CReward signals can be misaligned with evolutionary fitness — the pygmy three-toed sloth may be going extinct due to addiction to Valium-like compounds in its diet
  • DThe reward system breaks down in old age — illustrated by dementia
The brain's reward system is only a crude hint of what's good for evolutionary fitness. It can be "hacked" — through drugs, sugary drinks, or video game addiction. The pygmy three-toed sloth appears addicted to a Valium-like substance in its diet and may be going extinct. An entire species can disappear by satisfying its reward system in a maladaptive way.
Question 5
Russell argues that consciousness is irrelevant to the risks posed by AI. What analogy does he use?
  • AIf code will destroy humanity, learning it also produces consciousness doesn't change that prediction — "it's competence, not consciousness, that matters"
  • BA calculator doesn't need consciousness to solve math problems
  • CA self-driving car doesn't need to feel fear to brake
  • DAnimals can be dangerous without being self-aware
Russell says: suppose you analyze code that will destroy the human race. Then you learn it also creates machine consciousness. Does that change your prediction? Not at all. Hollywood plots about machines "mysteriously becoming conscious and hating humans" miss the point entirely.

Question 7
Why does Russell say comparing "machine IQ" to human IQ is nonsensical?
  • AMachines don't have brains so IQ doesn't apply
  • BHuman IQ correlates across cognitive activities, but machines have sharp boundaries of competence — like asking a horse to pole-vault
  • CIQ tests are culturally biased against machines
  • DMachines are too intelligent for IQ to be measured
"Trying to assign an IQ to machines is like trying to get four-legged animals to compete in a human decathlon. True, horses can run fast and jump high, but they have a lot of trouble with pole-vaulting and throwing the discus." AlphaZero is superhuman at Go, chess, and shogi but literally cannot drive a car or teach a class.
Question 8
Russell discusses four types of agent programs. Which type connects perception directly to action without any reasoning or deliberation?
  • AKnowledge-based agent
  • BReinforcement learning agent
  • CReflex agent
  • DUtility-maximizing agent
Reflex agents connect perception directly to action — like the human blinking reflex. The objective isn't represented in the agent; someone else has to decide everything in advance. Russell gives the example: "if then ." The reflex doesn't "know what it's doing."
Question 9
What happened when Google Photos' image-labeling AI made a classification error in 2015, and what deeper problem does this illustrate?
  • AIt labeled photos of Black people as "gorillas" — illustrating how a loss function that treats all misclassifications equally can produce catastrophically offensive results
  • BIt confused dogs and cats — illustrating the limits of deep learning
  • CIt mislabeled food items — illustrating the need for more training data
  • DIt confused celebrities — illustrating privacy concerns
The Google Photos labeling incident illustrates the critical difference between perceptual AI (providing probability estimates) and decision-making AI (taking actions with consequences). When AI labels images automatically, misclassification costs are not equal — the loss function must account for the real-world impact of different types of errors.
Question 10
Russell argues that research on "narrow AI" or "tool AI" often contributes to general-purpose AI. What example does he give to support this?
  • ASpam filters led to advances in natural language understanding
  • BAlphaGo's team improved general-purpose techniques (lookahead search + reinforcement learning) rather than writing Go-specific code — and AlphaZero then beat world-best programs at Go, chess, and shogi
  • CSelf-driving car research led to breakthroughs in robotics
  • DVoice assistants led to improvements in machine translation
The AlphaGo team didn't write Go-specific code. They improved two general-purpose techniques — lookahead search and reinforcement learning. AlphaZero then used those techniques to master Go, chess, and shogi in one day. Similarly, LeCun's work on handwritten digit recognition produced convolutional neural networks, which revolutionized multiple fields.
