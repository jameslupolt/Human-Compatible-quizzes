What Russell's book missed — and what it got right

The Problem Before Transformers

Before 2017, the best AI language systems used recurrent neural networks (RNNs). An RNN reads a sentence the way you read a ticker tape: one word at a time, left to right. It keeps a running summary of what it has read so far — like trying to remember a long story by keeping just one sticky note.

This had two crippling problems. First, the sticky note is too small: by the time you reach the end of a long paragraph, the details from the beginning have been smudged and overwritten. Second, you can't read faster by hiring more people — each word depends on the summary from the previous word, so the work is fundamentally serial. You can't parallelize it.

  • BRNNs could only process images, not text

RNNs must process tokens in strict sequence — each step depends on the last. This means (1) you can't use more hardware to go faster (no parallelism), and (2) information from early in a long sequence gets degraded as it's compressed through hundreds of serial steps. This is called the "vanishing gradient" problem. Transformers solved both issues at once.

The Core Idea: Attention

In 2017, a team at Google published "Attention Is All You Need" — one of the most important papers in the history of computing. Their key insight: throw away the ticker tape entirely.

Instead of reading left to right, a transformer looks at all the words at once and asks: "For each word, which other words are most relevant to understanding it?" This is called self-attention.

Think of it like a room full of people at a dinner party. In an RNN, you can only whisper to the person next to you, who whispers to the next. In a transformer, everyone can talk to everyone simultaneously. Each person decides who to pay attention to based on what they need to know.

Concretely: for the sentence "The animal didn't cross the street because it was too tired" — what does "it" refer to? A transformer computes an attention score between "it" and every other word, discovering that "it" should attend strongly to "animal" (not "street"). It does this through learned patterns of queries ("what am I looking for?"), keys ("what do I have to offer?"), and values ("what information do I carry?").

This is done through learned query-key-value projections — the model learns what patterns of attention are useful, rather than being told. Crucially, all of this happens in parallel, not sequentially.

Multiple Perspectives: Multi-Head Attention

One set of attention scores captures one kind of relationship (like "what does this pronoun refer to?"). But language has many kinds of relationships simultaneously — grammar, meaning, tone, factual associations, and more.

A transformer runs multiple attention "heads" in parallel — typically 12 to 128. Each head learns to look for a different kind of pattern. One head might track grammatical subject-verb agreement; another might track which entities are being compared; a third might notice that the tone shifted from formal to sarcastic.

These heads are then stacked into layers. Early layers tend to learn surface patterns (which words are near each other, basic syntax). Deeper layers compose these into higher-level abstractions (argument structure, factual reasoning, style). A large model might have 80+ layers, each with dozens of heads — a cascade of increasingly abstract pattern recognition.

Researchers have found that individual heads do specialize — some track syntax, some track coreference, some track positional patterns. This is all done in parallel (not slower), and stacking many layers lets these patterns compose into complex reasoning.

The Scaling Revelation

The transformer paper was published in June 2017 — two years before Russell's book. But in 2017, transformers were "just" a better translation model. Nobody yet knew the most important thing about them.

In 2020, researchers at OpenAI discovered scaling laws: transformer performance improves predictably and smoothly as you increase three things — model size (number of parameters), training data, and compute (number of calculations). Double all three and the model gets reliably better. There was no obvious ceiling.

This was revolutionary because previous AI approaches hit walls. Making an RNN bigger didn't reliably help. Making an expert system bigger made it more brittle. But transformers converted raw scale — something the tech industry is very good at providing — directly into capability.

It meant capability was, in a sense, purchasable: spend 10x more on compute, get a predictable improvement. This launched the modern AI arms race and made transformers the dominant architecture.

What Russell Wrote in 2019

In Chapter 2, Russell discusses deep learning and is candid about his skepticism: "My own view… is that it falls far short of what is needed" for human-level AI. He saw deep learning as producing powerful reflex agents — systems that map input to output without genuine understanding, reasoning, or planning.

This was a reasonable position in 2019. At that time, the largest language model (GPT-2, with 1.5 billion parameters) could write plausible paragraphs but quickly became incoherent. No language model could reliably answer questions, write code, reason about novel situations, or carry on a sustained conversation. The idea that making a text-prediction model bigger would produce something that seemed to reason would have struck most AI researchers as implausible.

Emergent Capabilities: The Big Surprise

The most unsettling discovery was emergence: abilities that appeared suddenly at certain scales without being explicitly trained. A model with 1 billion parameters couldn't do arithmetic. A model with 100 billion, trained on the same kind of data, suddenly could — not because anyone taught it arithmetic, but because predicting text well enough requires modeling the processes that generate text, including mathematical reasoning.

This is what Russell didn't anticipate. He assumed — along with most of the field — that getting from "pattern matcher" to "reasoner" would require fundamental new ideas, not just more of the same architecture with more data and compute. Whether LLMs truly "reason" or merely simulate reasoning convincingly enough to be useful remains deeply contested.

Why Almost Everyone Missed It

Russell wasn't alone. The transformer paper was published in 2017, but its consequences were not obvious until 2020-2022. Several things conspired to make this hard to predict:

1. The "bitter lesson" hadn't been fully absorbed. Rich Sutton's 2019 essay argued that general methods leveraging computation always win over human-knowledge-engineered approaches. Transformers were the purest vindication of this principle yet.

2. The relevant experience didn't exist. Nobody had ever trained a model with 175 billion parameters on hundreds of billions of words. You can't predict emergent behaviors from small-scale experiments.

3. "Next-word prediction" sounds trivial. The training objective sounds too simple to produce intelligence. But predicting what a human would write next requires modeling the world that humans write about — physics, psychology, logic, culture, and everything else.

What Russell Got Profoundly Right

Here is the irony: the transformer revolution has made Russell's core argument — about the standard model, value alignment, and the King Midas problem — more urgent, not less.

LLMs are trained to predict text (a fixed objective). They are then fine-tuned with reward models (another fixed objective). They are deployed to maximize engagement, revenue, or user satisfaction (more fixed objectives). Every one of these is a "purpose put into the machine" that may not be the purpose we really desire. The misalignment Russell warned about now operates at a scale and speed he didn't anticipate.

Putting It All Together: The Transformer Architecture

A full transformer stacks together several building blocks:

1. Tokenization: Text is split into "tokens" (roughly word pieces). "understanding" might become "under" + "standing".

2. Embedding: Each token is converted into a list of numbers (a vector) that represents its meaning in a high-dimensional space. Words with similar meanings end up near each other.

3. Positional encoding: Since the model sees all tokens at once (not sequentially), it needs to know where each token is. Position information is added to each embedding.

4. Multi-head self-attention + feed-forward layers: Repeated many times (the "layers" of the network). Each pass lets tokens exchange information and refine their representations.

5. Output: The final representation of the last token is used to predict a probability distribution over what token comes next.

Without positional encoding, the model couldn't distinguish "dog bites man" from "man bites dog" because the same tokens would produce the same attention patterns. Positional encodings inject order information, giving each position a unique signature that the model can learn to use.
