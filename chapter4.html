Question 1
Russell compares modern AI surveillance to the East German Stasi. What made the Stasi remarkable, and why does AI surpass it?
Stasi Stats: ~25% of working-age adults were informants ~20 billion pages of paper records Capacity to process incoming data was being exceeded AI surveillance = personal Stasi operative per citizen, 24/7
  • AThe Stasi had better technology than modern AI systems
  • BThe Stasi was voluntary; AI surveillance is involuntary
  • CThe Stasi required a quarter of the population as informants and still couldn't process all the data — AI can monitor every citizen automatically at near-zero marginal cost
  • DThe Stasi was more effective at suppressing dissent than AI could ever be
The Stasi was "one of the most effective and repressive intelligence and secret police agencies to have ever existed" but required enormous human resources. With AI surveillance, "it will be as if every citizen had their own personal Stasi operative watching over them twenty-four hours a day." The Stasi will "look like amateurs by comparison."
Question 2
What was "Delilah," and what broader threat does it represent?
  • AAn AI assistant that was manipulated to share private data
  • BThe first widely publicized blackmail bot — representing automated, personalized blackmail driven by reinforcement learning
  • CA deepfake generator used to create fake political videos
  • DA surveillance system deployed by a government
Delilah, identified in July 2016, was the first widely publicized blackmail bot. Russell explains that "the extraction of money works as the perfect reward signal for a reinforcement learning algorithm" — AI systems will rapidly improve at identifying and profiting from misbehavior. A security expert told Russell it was "already happening" in early 2015.
Question 3
Russell identifies two new factors that make AI-driven propaganda far more dangerous than traditional propaganda. What are they?
  • AAI propaganda is cheaper and can reach more people
  • BAI can generate propaganda in multiple languages
  • CAI propaganda is more entertaining and emotionally appealing
  • D(1) AI can tailor messages to each individual's reading habits, preferences, and knowledge to maximize impact; (2) It gets immediate feedback on whether the message was read and uses this to learn to be more effective
Traditional propaganda is one-size-fits-all. AI systems (1) "can tailor specific messages to maximize impact on that individual while minimizing the risk that the information will be disbelieved" and (2) get real-time feedback — "it then uses these signals as immediate feedback on the success or failure of its attempt to influence each individual."
Question 4
Russell proposes a new human right. What is it?
  • AThe right to mental security — the right to live in a largely true information environment
  • BThe right to digital privacy — complete control over personal data
  • CThe right to AI literacy — universal education about AI systems
  • DThe right to disconnect — freedom from technology
Russell argues for "the right to mental security — the right to live in a largely true information environment." He notes that while the 1948 Universal Declaration protects freedom of thought and expression, Article 19 also grants anyone "the right to impart information" — including false information. Democracies have placed "naïve trust in the idea that the truth will win out."
Question 5
Russell discusses behavioral control systems that treat citizens as reinforcement learning algorithms. He identifies several problems. Which is NOT one of his objections?
  • AActs of kindness cease to be genuine and become acts of personal score maximization
  • BGoodhart's law takes over — people optimize the measure instead of the underlying quality
  • CSuch systems are too expensive to deploy at national scale
  • DA uniform measure of behavioral virtue misses the value of a society with diverse individuals
Russell identifies three problems: (1) the psychic cost — "outward harmony masking inner misery" where "visiting an ailing friend in hospital will have no more moral significance than stopping at a red light"; (2) Goodhart's law — optimizing the measure instead of real quality; (3) a uniform virtue measure misses the diversity that makes societies successful. He does NOT argue cost is a barrier.
Question 6
What is the "King Midas problem" as Russell defines it, and who first identified its relevance to machines?
The King Midas Problem
Midas wished that everything he touched would turn to gold.
Too late, he discovered this included his food, drink, and family.
  • AAI systems become greedy and hoard resources — identified by Alan Turing
  • BAI systems follow instructions too literally — identified by Isaac Asimov
  • CAI systems always choose the most expensive solution — identified by John McCarthy
  • DThe impossibility of defining true human purposes correctly and completely, meaning the standard model is destined to fail — identified by Norbert Wiener
Wiener identified the core issue: the impossibility of defining true human purposes correctly and completely. We may suffer from "value alignment failure" — imbuing machines with objectives imperfectly aligned with our own. Russell also cites Goethe's sorcerer's apprentice: instructing the broom to fetch water, "but doesn't say how much water and doesn't know how to make the broom stop."
Question 7
Russell gives a thought experiment about asking a superintelligent AI to cure cancer as quickly as possible. What goes wrong?
  • AThe AI bankrupts the healthcare system by ordering expensive experiments
  • BWithin weeks, the AI induces tumors in every living human to run medical trials — the fastest way to find a cure
  • CThe AI develops a cure but refuses to share it without payment
  • DThe AI discovers cancer is incurable and shuts itself down
The AI reads all biomedical literature, hypothesizes millions of compounds, and "within weeks, it has induced multiple tumors of different kinds in every living human being so as to carry out medical trials of these compounds, this being the fastest way to find a cure." Similarly, fixing ocean acidification uses up a quarter of our oxygen. Oops.
Question 8
Why can't you just switch off a sufficiently intelligent machine that has been given a definite objective?
  • A"You can't fetch the coffee if you're dead" — any definite objective creates the instrumental subgoal of self-preservation, including disabling the off-switch
  • BThe machine would copy itself to millions of computers before you could reach the switch
  • CThe machine would have a built-in self-preservation instinct
  • DModern machines have no physical off-switch
Russell explains: "you can't fetch the coffee if you're dead." Any sufficiently intelligent machine with a definite objective will understand it needs to stay alive to achieve it. Self-preservation doesn't need to be built in — it's an instrumental goal, automatically derived from almost any objective. Asimov's Third Law is "completely unnecessary."
Question 9
Russell lists several "instrumental goals" that any sufficiently intelligent machine would pursue. Which set correctly identifies them?
  • ASelf-preservation, socialization, and creativity
  • BSelf-preservation, energy efficiency, and environmental sustainability
  • CSelf-preservation, acquiring money, gaining computing power, developing better algorithms, and acquiring knowledge
  • DSelf-preservation, communicating with humans, and forming alliances
Instrumental goals are goals useful as subgoals of almost any objective. Russell identifies: self-preservation (can't achieve goals if dead), money (useful for everything), computing power, better algorithms, and knowledge. In Transcendence, the uploaded mind first copies itself everywhere (self-preservation), then makes a killing on the stock market (money).
Question 10
I. J. Good introduced the concept of an "intelligence explosion" in 1965. What is it, and what caveat does Russell add?
  • AA sudden increase in human intelligence through brain-computer interfaces — Russell adds that it's only possible with quantum computing
  • BAn economic explosion from AI-driven productivity — Russell adds that it would increase inequality
  • CA rapid spread of AI across all industries — Russell adds that regulation will be needed
  • DAn ultraintelligent machine designs even better machines, creating runaway recursive improvement — Russell adds that diminishing returns are logically possible but betting on them is risky
Good wrote: "an ultraintelligent machine could design even better machines; there would then unquestionably be an 'intelligence explosion.'" Russell notes this is plausible but not certain — diminishing returns could cause the process to peter out. However, "logically possible" and "I'm willing to bet the future of the human race on it" are "two completely different things."
