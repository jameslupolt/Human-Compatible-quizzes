Question 1
Russell lists five candidates for "the biggest event in the future of humanity." Which did he argue would win?
  • AWe all live forever (medical solution to aging)
  • BWe are visited by a superior alien civilization
  • CWe invent superintelligent AI
  • DWe invent faster-than-light travel and conquer the universe
Superintelligent AI would be the winner because it could help us achieve all the others — avoid catastrophes, achieve eternal life, and faster-than-light travel, if possible. Russell called it analogous to the arrival of a superior alien civilization, but much more likely.

1956 — Summer program that launched AI
1995 — Russell & Norvig publish AI: A Modern Approach
2016 — AlphaGo defeats Lee Sedol
  • AMIT — organized by Norbert Wiener and Alan Turing
  • BDartmouth College — organized by John McCarthy and Marvin Minsky (with Shannon and Rochester)
  • CStanford — organized by John McCarthy and Herbert Simon
  • DPrinceton — organized by Claude Shannon and Alan Turing
McCarthy and Minsky persuaded Claude Shannon and Nathaniel Rochester to co-organize the 1956 Dartmouth summer program. They proposed that "a significant advance can be made… if a carefully selected group of scientists work on it together for a summer." It took much longer than a summer.
Question 3
Russell tells the story of Lord Rutherford and Leo Szilard to illustrate what point?
Sept 11, 1933: Rutherford declares atomic energy is "moonshine" Sept 12, 1933: Szilard reads the report over breakfast Same day: Szilard invents the nuclear chain reaction Time from "impossible" to "solved": < 24 hours
  • ANuclear physics is more dangerous than AI
  • BExperts are always wrong about scientific progress
  • CAI breakthroughs require military funding
  • DBetting against human ingenuity is foolhardy — breakthroughs can happen overnight
The problem of liberating nuclear energy went from "impossible" to essentially solved in less than 24 hours. Russell uses this to warn against a "denialism" in the AI community that bets against AI ever reaching human-level intelligence.
Question 4
What is the "standard model" of AI as Russell defines it?
  • AMachines are intelligent to the extent that their actions can be expected to achieve their objectives
  • BMachines should be designed to mimic the structure of the human brain
  • CAI systems should pass the Turing test to be considered intelligent
  • DMachines should pursue a set of programmed ethical rules
The standard model defines intelligence as optimizing a given objective. Humans give machines objectives, and the machines pursue them. Russell argues this widespread scheme — shared by AI, control theory, economics, and statistics — is fundamentally flawed.
Question 5
Who warned in 1960 that "we had better be quite sure that the purpose put into the machine is the purpose which we really desire"?
  • AAlan Turing
  • BJohn McCarthy
  • CNorbert Wiener
  • DMarvin Minsky
Norbert Wiener, a legendary MIT mathematician, wrote "Some Moral and Technical Consequences of Automation" in 1960 after seeing Arthur Samuel's checker-playing program learn to beat its creator. He identified the core danger of the standard model decades before anyone else took it seriously.
Question 6
Russell describes how content-selection algorithms on social media illustrate the problem with the standard model. What do these algorithms optimize for, and what is the unintended consequence?
  • AThey optimize for user satisfaction, leading to filter bubbles
  • BThey optimize for click-through, which drives users toward extreme views because extreme users are more predictable
  • CThey optimize for time on site, accidentally creating addiction
  • DThey optimize for ad revenue by showing only political content
The algorithm learns to make users more predictable so it can show items they're likely to click. People with extreme political views are more predictable. Russell says the consequences include "the resurgence of fascism" and threats to democracies, the EU, and NATO — "not bad for a few lines of code."

The joke captures how our response to the arrival of superintelligent AI has been "underwhelming" — we're essentially not paying attention to what could be the biggest event in human history.
Question 8
Russell cites the ancient motif of "three wishes" stories. What principle do they illustrate about AI?
  • AAI systems should be limited to three goals at a time
  • BGetting exactly what you ask for can be disastrous — the third wish always undoes the first two
  • CHumans are inherently bad at wishing for what they really want
  • DAI should grant user requests only after three confirmations
In every three-wishes story, the third wish undoes the first two. This illustrates that if we put the wrong objective into a more-intelligent-than-us machine, it achieves that objective — and we lose. We've known this peril "for thousands of years."
Question 9
Russell proposes a revised definition of what makes machines beneficial. Fill in the blank: "Machines are beneficial to the extent that their actions can be expected to achieve ___."
  • Atheir objectives
  • Boptimal outcomes
  • Csafe behavior
  • Dour objectives
The key shift is from "their objectives" (the standard model) to "our objectives." Because machines will inevitably be uncertain about our objectives, they will defer to humans, ask permission, accept correction, and allow themselves to be switched off — uncertainty is "a feature, not a bug."
