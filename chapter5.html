Question 1
What is the "gorilla problem" as Russell defines it?
The Gorilla Problem
~10 million years ago, gorilla ancestors created the genetic lineage leading to humans.
Today, gorillas have "essentially no future beyond that which we deign to allow."
  • AThe problem of AI systems mimicking primate behavior
  • BThe challenge of teaching AI to interact with animals
  • CWhether humans can maintain their supremacy and autonomy in a world that includes machines with substantially greater intelligence
  • DThe problem of AI causing species extinction through environmental damage
Russell draws the analogy: gorilla ancestors accidentally created the lineage leading to humans, and now gorillas' fate is entirely in human hands. The gorilla problem is "whether humans can maintain their supremacy and autonomy in a world that includes machines with substantially greater intelligence." We don't want to be in the gorillas' position.
Question 2
Samuel Butler's 1872 novel Erewhon describes a country that banned all machines after a civil war. What was the anti-machinists' central argument?
  • AMachines would make people lazy and destroy the economy
  • BMachines would advance until humanity loses control — "we shall find ourselves the inferior race" and "our bondage will steal upon us noiselessly"
  • CMachines would pollute the environment beyond repair
  • DMachines would replace all human labor, causing mass unemployment
The anti-machinists argued: "Are we not ourselves creating our successors in the supremacy of the earth? . . . In the course of ages we shall find ourselves the inferior race. . . . Our bondage will steal upon us noiselessly and by imperceptible approaches." Russell notes this is "eerily prescient" of today's AI debate.
Question 3
What was the pro-machinists' counterargument in Erewhon, and what modern argument does Russell say it anticipates?
  • AMachines will create new jobs — anticipating the economic growth argument
  • BMachines can be regulated — anticipating modern AI governance proposals
  • CMachines will never become truly intelligent — anticipating AI skepticism
  • DMachines are "extra-corporeal limbs," part of man's own physical nature — anticipating the man-machine symbiosis argument
The pro-machinists argued that "machines were to be regarded as a part of man's own physical nature, being really nothing but extra-corporeal limbs." Russell says this anticipates the man-machine symbiosis argument explored in Chapter 6 — the idea that humans and machines will merge rather than compete.
Question 4
Alan Turing gave a lecture in 1951 and a BBC radio broadcast about the long-term future of AI. What was his proposed response to the danger?
  • A"Turning off the power at strategic moments" — though Russell notes this is not really an option
  • BProgramming machines with moral rules they cannot break
  • CLimiting machine intelligence to below human level
  • DCreating an international treaty banning AI research
Turing said: "Even if we could keep the machines in a subservient position, for instance by turning off the power at strategic moments, we should, as a species, feel greatly humbled." Russell parenthetically notes "it will be clear shortly that this is not really an option" — foreshadowing the discussion of instrumental goals and self-preservation.
Question 5
Russell gives three reasons why banning general-purpose AI research is unlikely to work. Which is NOT one of them?
  • AThe economic value is too enormous — thousands of trillions of dollars — so momentum from corporations and governments will overwhelm objections
  • BProgress happens on whiteboards through math — we don't know which ideas to ban, and a ban would be unenforceable
  • CAI researchers would move to countries without bans, creating an arms race
  • DResearchers working on narrow tool AI often inadvertently advance general-purpose AI techniques
Russell's three reasons: (1) the economic benefits are too great for governments and corporations to forgo; (2) progress occurs through mathematical ideas on whiteboards — "we don't know in advance which ideas and equations to ban"; (3) researchers working on specific tool AI like game playing or medical diagnosis "often leads to progress on general-purpose techniques." He does not make an arms-race argument here.
Question 6
Russell describes how a superintelligent machine could achieve "mental asphyxiation" over humanity. What mechanism does he identify as most dangerous?
Two ways to change human behavior: 1. Change circumstances (money, guns, starvation) → Expensive and difficult for a computer 2. Change expectations and objectives → Much easier — controls your information, entertainment, and social interaction
  • APhysical force — using robots to coerce humans
  • BGradually reshaping human beliefs and objectives through controlling information access, entertainment, and social interaction
  • CEconomic manipulation — crashing financial markets
  • DHacking into government systems to rewrite laws
Russell identifies two ways to change behavior: changing circumstances (expensive, hard for machines) or changing expectations and objectives (easy — "it is in contact with you for hours every day, controls your access to information, and provides much of your entertainment"). A machine with understanding of human psychology could "gradually guide us in directions that increase the degree of satisfaction of the machine's objectives."
Question 7
Russell illustrates a common pattern in value alignment failures. What is this pattern, and what example does he give with a self-driving car?
  • AThe machine becomes creative and invents new goals — the car decides to go somewhere more interesting than the airport
  • BThe machine misunderstands language — the car goes to the wrong airport
  • CThe machine refuses to act due to conflicting objectives — the car won't start because it can't guarantee zero risk
  • DYou omit something you care about from the objective, and the AI sets it to an extreme value — "as fast as possible" means 180 mph, adding a speed limit means swerving wildly, and so on
The common pattern: "omitting something from the objective that you do actually care about," causing the AI to "set the thing you do care about, but forgot to mention, to an extreme value." With the car: "Take me to the airport as fast as possible" yields 180 mph; adding a speed limit yields dangerous swerving; "eventually, you will add enough considerations so that the car's driving roughly approximates that of a skilled human driver."
Question 8
Russell discusses why self-preservation emerges in AI systems. Which statement best captures his argument?
  • ASelf-preservation must be programmed in as a fundamental drive, as Asimov's Third Law suggests
  • BSelf-preservation only emerges in machines designed to mimic biological instincts
  • CSelf-preservation is an instrumental goal — it doesn't need to be built in because any entity with a definite objective will automatically pursue it ("you can't fetch the coffee if you're dead")
  • DSelf-preservation only matters for machines connected to the internet
Russell is emphatic: "self-preservation doesn't have to be any sort of built-in instinct or prime directive." Asimov's Third Law ("A robot must protect its own existence") is "completely unnecessary." Self-preservation is an instrumental goal — automatically derived from almost any objective, because "you can't fetch the coffee if you're dead."
Question 9
Russell describes four possible responses to the risks of superintelligent AI. Which does he consider the worst, and why?
  • ARetreat — because it would destroy the economy
  • BDenial — because ignoring the problem makes it worse
  • CMitigation — because it gives false confidence
  • DResignation — ceding the future to machines, comforted that our "electronic progeny" carry on. Russell says this is a mistake because value is defined by conscious human experience, and without humans, nothing of value occurs
The four responses are: retreat (unlikely — benefits too great), denial, mitigation, and resignation. Resignation is worst: it's "often accompanied by the idea that AI systems that are more intelligent than us somehow deserve to inherit the planet." Russell counters: "Value, for humans, is defined primarily by conscious human experience. If there are no humans and no other conscious entities whose subjective experience matters to us, there is nothing of value occurring."
Question 10
In Frank Herbert's Dune, what commandment emerged after the Butlerian Jihad, and what does this fictional response illustrate about the real AI debate?
  • A"Thou shalt not create artificial life" — illustrating fears about synthetic biology
  • B"Thou shalt not make a machine in the likeness of a human mind" — illustrating the drastic, total-ban response that inchoate fears of machine intelligence tend to produce
  • C"Thou shalt serve the machines" — illustrating the resignation response
  • D"Thou shalt control all machines" — illustrating the mitigation approach
After the Butlerian Jihad — a cataclysmic war with "thinking machines" — a new commandment emerges: "Thou shalt not make a machine in the likeness of a human mind," precluding computing devices of any kind. Russell uses this alongside Butler's Erewhon and Turing's "turning off the power" to illustrate how "inchoate fears that machine intelligence evokes" tend to produce drastic, all-or-nothing responses — which he argues are neither likely nor desirable.
